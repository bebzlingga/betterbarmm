## The opportunity

Every day, people across the Bangsamoro travel to an office, queue, and are told to come back with one more document. Every day, staff behind the counter retype forms, search for files and assemble the same reports by hand. Artificial intelligence and automation can change both: services that answer at any hour and move a request along on their own, and offices that spend less time on paperwork and more on people.

The Bangsamoro Government already has a place to start. A national law tells government offices, local governments included, to go digital; the region has digital service centers in a number of towns and its own citizen app, [BGov](https://bgov.bangsamoro.gov.ph/). This explainer sets out how AI could improve service delivery and the work behind it, what AI does well and what it does not, and what the region would need to put in place, from connectivity to a regional law of its own.

## Technology no longer waits

For most of the last century, a new technology took a generation to reach most people. A government could plan a change in five-year cycles and still be on time.

That is no longer how it works. When ChatGPT was released at the end of November 2022, it reached about 100 million monthly users in two months, the fastest of any consumer app on record. TikTok had taken about nine months to get there, and Instagram about two and a half years ([Reuters, via Inquirer](https://business.inquirer.net/384799/chatgpt-sets-record-for-fastest-growing-user-base-analyst-note)).

The tools themselves now change in months. Stanford University's 2025 AI Index found that AI scores on hard new tests rose by up to 67 percentage points in a single year, and that the cost of running a system as capable as the 2022 ChatGPT fell more than 280-fold in about two years ([Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report)). An improvement that once took years of research can arrive in an update between one month and the next.

{{figure:pace}}

The rest of the country is moving with it. The [E-Governance Act](https://www.lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2025/ra_12254_2025.html), Republic Act No. 12254, signed on September 5, 2025, tells every government office, local governments included, to move its services online. A local government that has not built its own portal within a year must use the national eLGU system (Section 9). The Department of Information and Communications Technology has announced AI assistants for 50,000 public servants to begin with, and plans to let citizens ask for services by speaking or typing in their own language ([GovInsider](https://govinsider.asia/intl-en/article/philippines-dict-to-integrate-ai-agents-in-the-governments-digital-platforms)).

A region that does not adapt does not stay where it is. Its people and businesses already use these tools on their phones. Other governments will answer in minutes what a Bangsamoro office still answers in weeks, and the distance grows every month, because the technology does not pause while a plan is written.

## What AI is, and what it is not

**Automation** is a machine doing the same steps the same way every time: a text message when a permit is ready, a form that will not submit until every field is filled. **Artificial intelligence** is software that has learned from very large numbers of examples, so it can handle what fixed rules cannot: a question typed in everyday words, a handwritten form, a 300-page report that needs a summary. It predicts what a good answer looks like, and it is usually, but not always, right.

{{figure:robot}}

AI is fast, tireless and consistent. People bring judgment, local knowledge, care and accountability. AI can also state something false with complete confidence, repeat the gaps in its data, and work less well in Maguindanaon, Meranaw, Tausug or Sinama than in English. The answer is to pair them: AI reads, sorts, drafts and counts; a person decides.

## Better service at the counter

Service delivery is where people feel government. AI and automation could change it in plain, practical ways.

- **One door, at any hour.** Apply, pay and follow a request online, from home or a Digital Bangsamoro center, instead of traveling to Cotabato City and queueing. The regional government's [BGov](https://bgov.bangsamoro.gov.ph/) app is built to be that door: one digital identity, verified once with a government ID, for services across its ministries. An assistant inside it could answer questions on requirements, fees and schedules day and night, in the languages people speak.
- **Right the first time.** An application can be checked the moment it arrives, and the applicant told at once what is missing, instead of finding out on a second visit.
- **No more "come back next week."** Automatic status updates by text or email tell people where their request stands and when to collect it.
- **Deadlines kept.** The [Ease of Doing Business Act](https://www.lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2018/ra_11032_2018.html) gives an office 3 working days for a simple transaction, 7 for a complex one and 20 for a highly technical one. Automating the routine steps leaves staff time for the parts that need judgment.
- **Help that reaches the islands.** For towns in Tawi-Tawi, Basilan and the Special Geographic Area, far from regional offices, services that can be done online matter most.
- **Faster in a crisis.** After a flood, AI can read reports from the field and show within hours which barangays have asked for help, and which have not been heard from.

## Better work behind the counter

Most of a government's effort never reaches the counter: files, letters, payroll, purchases, reports. This is where automation often saves the most, and where mistakes cost citizens least.

- **Paperwork and records.** Scanned archives turned into searchable text, so any document can be found in seconds; memos and documents routed and tracked instead of carried from desk to desk.
- **Drafting.** First drafts of letters, minutes, briefings and translations, which staff then check and sign.
- **Money.** Spending compared with the budget every month, money left unspent flagged before the year ends, and routine financial reports assembled automatically.
- **Procurement.** Bids checked for missing documents, prices compared with what other offices paid for the same thing, and unusual patterns, such as the same supplier winning again and again, flagged for a second look.
- **People.** Attendance, leave and payroll checked for errors, and training needs found from what staff actually do.
- **Planning and monitoring.** Data from different ministries joined up, and project reports, photos and locations read to show what has been built and what is late.

The gains add up twice. Every hour saved behind the counter is an hour staff can spend with the public, and every record put in order makes the next service easier to automate.

## From records to insight

Underneath all of this is one skill: reading many records at once and finding the pattern. BetterBARMM's Incident Watch shows how it works. With AI tools, we read 798 news reports from 2019 to 2026, merged them into 264 incidents, placed 251 on the map and sorted each by kind, in minutes rather than weeks.

{{figure:insight}}

The result makes one pattern plain: about two in every three incidents the region's news reported were shootings. It also shows the limit. Only 7 road crashes appear, because most never make the news. A pattern in the data is a pattern in what was recorded, and a person has to know the difference.

## Where the region starts

{{figure:readiness}}

The groundwork has started. Under the LeAPS program, the Ministry of the Interior and Local Government and the United Nations Development Programme have opened Digital Bangsamoro centers in at least 8 towns; as of January 2025, 4 of the 14 e-services planned for its web portal were online ([UNDP](https://www.undp.org/philippines/press-releases/leaps-digital-center-milg-barmm-welcomes-clients-expands-e-services-government-employees); [UNGM](https://www.ungm.org/Public/Notice/263790)). The Bangsamoro Government has also launched [BGov](https://bgov.bangsamoro.gov.ph/), an Android app that gives each resident one digital identity to use across BARMM ministries. For now it carries official announcements and lets people submit and track selected requests; aid and welfare (AMBaG), a BARMM job portal and the AHME scholarship are due between late 2026 and early 2027, and an iPhone version in 2027. In October 2026, Google Play listed more than 1,000 downloads ([BGov](https://bgov.bangsamoro.gov.ph/); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=ph.gov.bangsamoro.lifecert)). In 2025 the Bangsamoro Planning and Development Authority and the Philippine Institute for Development Studies held a forum on "Reimagining Governance in the Age of AI," where the authority's director general said AI must be applied "with fairness, inclusivity, and accountability to ensure that no one is left behind" ([PIDS](https://pids.gov.ph/details/news/press-releases/ai-must-empower-not-exclude-barmm-communities)).

The gaps are larger. The Bangsamoro has no digital-governance law of its own: [Parliament Bill No. 116](https://legislation.betterbarmm.com/bills/116), to put the regional government's frontline services online, was filed in 2022 and never went past first reading. Only 27.7% of households in BARMM have internet, against 48.8% nationally, PIDS reported, and in 2024 the region had the slowest download speeds in the country ([Philstar](https://www.philstar.com/business/2025/06/13/2450101/dict-eyes-21k-free-wifi-sites-year)). Many records exist only as scans or Facebook posts. And we could find no regional policy on how Bangsamoro offices may use AI.

## What it will take

1. **Connect through partners, not only new infrastructure.** People already reach the internet in many ways: mobile data on their phones, satellite links where cables do not reach, free public Wi-Fi, schools and the Digital Bangsamoro centers. Rather than spend heavily on networks of its own, the region gets further through partnerships: with the DICT, whose Free Wi-Fi for All program had set up 586 sites in the region by May 2025 and aims for 50,000 nationwide by 2028 ([Philstar](https://www.philstar.com/business/2025/06/13/2450101/dict-eyes-21k-free-wifi-sites-year)); with telecoms companies, and with the small local internet providers that the [Konektadong Pinoy Act](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2025/ra_12234_2025.html) of 2025 now lets register with the National Telecommunications Commission instead of waiting for a franchise from Congress; and with towns, schools and development partners, as the Digital Bangsamoro centers were built with UNDP. The region's own money then goes where only it can: services that work on a basic phone and a weak signal, wherever people connect from.
2. **Put the records in order.** Machine-readable data, one standard code for every office, town and barangay, and a date on everything. AI is only as good as what it is given.
3. **Start behind the counter, and small.** Searching archives, drafting, checking forms and tracking documents carry little risk and pay back quickly. Decisions about benefits, permits or people should come much later, if at all.
4. **Keep a person in charge, and say where AI is used.** A named official decides and signs, and citizens can always reach a person instead.
5. **Protect personal data.** The [Data Privacy Act](https://www.lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html) applies to every system, and the E-Governance Act requires privacy impact assessments and privacy by design (Sections 10 and 24).
6. **Train staff, and keep them.** PIDS warned that the biggest risk is job losses among entry-level workers. The aim is to move people from repetitive work to the work only people can do.
7. **Write the rules down.** A Bangsamoro law on digital services, as [Parliament Bill No. 116](https://legislation.betterbarmm.com/bills/116) proposed, and a regional policy on AI in government, set before the tools spread rather than after.

## The cost of waiting

AI will not fix a service that is broken for other reasons, and it will make mistakes. But the region does not get to choose whether AI arrives: its people already carry it in their pockets, and the national government is building it into the services they use. What the Bangsamoro Government can still choose is whether to use it on its own terms, in its own languages, on its own records and under its own rules, to give its people faster and simpler service. Starting is cheapest now.
