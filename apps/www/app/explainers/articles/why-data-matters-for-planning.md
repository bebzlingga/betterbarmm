## A plan is only as good as its numbers

A government plans the way a family budgets: it needs to know what it has, what it needs, and what last month's spending bought. For a region of hundreds of towns, islands and barangays, that knowledge comes from data. How many families live below the poverty line, and where. Which barangays flood. Which towns have no health station. How many roads, classrooms and water systems were promised, and how many were built.

When those numbers are current, money can follow need. When they are old, missing or contradict each other, a plan is built on guesses: a health station goes where the population was ten years ago, relief misses the barangays that flood most, and nobody can tell at the end of the year whether a program worked.

{{figure:cycle}}

The [2nd Bangsamoro Development Plan 2023–2028](https://betterbarmm.com/development-plan) sets 62 targets across 16 areas, from poverty and jobs to farms, forests, health and peace. Each one can only be checked if someone counts the same thing again, the same way, and publishes it.

## What up-to-date looks like

Some of the region's numbers are already current, and they show what good looks like.

- **Town finances.** Every province, city and town reports what it collected and spent to the national Bureau of Local Government Finance, year after year. The [Local Government](https://lgu.betterbarmm.com) workspace holds those reports up to 2025, so any town's income and spending can be followed for a decade.
- **Procurement.** Every bid notice a regional office posts goes on PhilGEPS, the government's procurement system, as it happens. The [Procurement](https://procurement.betterbarmm.com) workspace holds more than 100,000 of them since 2019, with who won each contract.
- **Schools.** In 2025, the Department of Education began publishing [school-level files](https://www.deped.gov.ph/machine-ready-files/) anyone can download: every school, its learners for every school year up to 2025–26, its classrooms and its staff. Before that, the newest full list of the region's public schools this site could find was from 2019.

What the three have in common: one office counts the same thing every period, publishes it where anyone can find it, and ties each row to a code (a town's place code, a school's ID) so this year can be compared with last.

## How old the numbers are

Elsewhere, the figures a planner can find are years old, or not in public at all.

{{figure:ages}}

The plan's targets are checked against figures from 2024 and 2025; none yet from 2026. The count of health facilities by town comes from the Department of Health's registry as of January 2021. The barangay populations behind flood and evacuation planning are from the 2020 census. Several of the plan's own baselines still include Sulu, which left the region in 2024. And no office keeps a public register of road crashes, drownings and fires: [Incident Watch](https://betterbarmm.com/accidents) has to read them from the news.

## The real problem: numbers years apart

Old figures are one problem. The bigger one is that the figures a plan needs are from different years, and a plan has to put them together.

You cannot plan for a town in 2026 with data from 2020. In six years its children have grown, families have moved, and in parts of the region even the borders have changed. That holds for every sector at once: a town's schools, health stations, roads, water and disaster plans all need numbers from the same year, because the plan has to weigh them against each other.

A plan rarely uses a number on its own. It divides one by another: people per health facility, learners per classroom, the poor per barangay, peso spent per family served. Each of those takes two or three records, and in the Bangsamoro they are years apart. Learners per school come from 2025–26, health facilities from 2021, barangay populations from 2020, the plan's readings from 2024 and 2025. A ratio of a 2025 count over a 2020 count describes no real year at all.

{{figure:apart}}

The region's map has also moved while the figures sat still. Maguindanao split into Maguindanao del Norte and del Sur in 2022. Sulu left the region in 2024, and the same year the Special Geographic Area's 8 towns were created from barangays that had belonged to North Cotabato. A 2020 census, a 2021 registry or a 2020 audit counts places by the old lines. To compare them with a 2025 figure, someone has to redraw each old count onto the new map, barangay by barangay, before the arithmetic can even start. Most planners do not have the time, so the numbers are used as they are, and the plan inherits the error.

The fix is not one new survey. It is counting the main things on the same cycle, so that each year's plan can draw its figures from the same year, on the same map.

## When the numbers disagree

Current is not enough if the numbers do not agree. Ask a simple question about one sector: how many learners were in school in the Bangsamoro in school year 2023–24? The official answers differ by nearly 200,000.

{{figure:three-counts}}

None of the three is wrong. Each counts something different. But only one comes with the schools it adds up, so only that one can be checked, broken down by town, or compared with next year. The same problem runs through other sectors. The plan's target for child stunting comes from health-office weighing records, and its latest figure from a national survey, so the two cannot be compared. A poverty baseline that counts Sulu cannot be compared with a reading that does not.

A figure in a speech, a press release or a poster can be valuable, and sometimes it is found nowhere else. But it cannot be added up, joined to a town or checked against last year's.

## What the gaps cost

When numbers are kept but not published, problems surface late, and usually in an audit or a budget hearing rather than in a register anyone could have checked.

{{figure:audits}}

The audits of the region's towns are a measure of their records. Of the 55 town audits for FY 2020 that this site could read, only 2 got a clean opinion. In 35, the auditors' main findings mention the inventory of the town's own property or supplies: a town that cannot count what it owns cannot plan what it needs. No report on a regional ministry could be read for any year from 2019 to 2024.

Budget reviews tell the same story. In May 2026, the Bangsamoro Parliament's Committee on Finance, Budget and Management reviewed the education budget and found shortfalls no public register had shown before ([Daily Tribune](https://tribune.net.ph/2026/05/28/barmm-budget-review-exposes-gaps-in-bangsamoro-education-sector), [Newsline](https://newsline.ph/audit-exposes-deepening-education-crisis-in-barmm/)).

{{figure:gaps}}

Each of those is a planning question, and every sector has its own version. How many health stations lack a midwife? Which barangays have no evacuation site? Which roads were paid for and not finished? With a register that counts them every year, place by place, the answer is a search away, and a shortfall is caught in March instead of a hearing in May.

## How far the region has to climb

Turning a number someone has into a number everyone can use takes five steps. The region has started on some, and not yet on others.

{{figure:ladder}}

None of these needs new technology. They need decisions:

- **Count the same things every year,** with the same definitions, so this year can be compared with last.
- **Publish what is counted,** in files people can download, not only in speeches, releases and posters.
- **Date every figure,** and say what it covers: which year, which places, whether Sulu is in it.
- **Use the codes that already exist,** the Philippine Statistics Authority's place codes and IDs like the school ID, so records from different offices line up.
- **Say how the data may be reused,** with clear terms, so citizens, researchers and other offices can build on it.

National agencies have shown it can be done quickly. The Department of Education went from reports to downloadable school files in a single release, and every town's finances already reach the Bureau of Local Government Finance each year. The region can do the same for what only it holds: its ministries' programs and spending, its madaris and learning centres, its scholarships, its projects under construction, its own audits.

## What BetterBARMM does, and does not do

BetterBARMM gathers what the region's offices and the national government publish, puts it side by side, and links every figure back to its source. It does not make the figures: every number here is the source's own, shown as published, with its date. Where a record is old, missing or in disagreement, we say so, as this explainer does.

The faster the region publishes, the more current this site can be. Every dataset an office opens is one more question a resident, a farmer, a teacher or a planner can answer for themselves.
