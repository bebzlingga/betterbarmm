## A plan is only as good as its numbers

A government plans the way a family budgets: it needs to know what it has, what it needs, and what last month's spending bought. For a region of hundreds of towns, islands and barangays, that knowledge comes from data. How many families live below the poverty line, and where. Which barangays flood. Which towns have no health station. How many roads, classrooms and water systems were promised, and how many were built.

The plans made on those numbers decide which towns get the next clinic, road, classroom or water system, and which do not. When the numbers are current, money can follow need. When they are old, missing or contradict each other, a plan is built on guesses: a health station goes where the population was years ago, relief misses the barangays that flood most, and nobody can tell at the end of the year whether a program worked.

{{figure:cycle}}

The [2nd Bangsamoro Development Plan 2023–2028](https://betterbarmm.com/development-plan) sets 62 targets across 16 areas, from poverty and jobs to farms, forests, health and peace. Each one can only be checked if someone counts the same thing again, the same way, and publishes it.

## What the numbers show

Good data does three jobs for a plan, and each one shapes what the region becomes.

- **It shows what is lacking.** Counted town by town, it points to the gaps: the barangays with no evacuation site, the towns with no health station, the schools with more learners than classrooms. A need that is not counted is a need that is not planned for.
- **It shows what to adjust.** Counted again the next year, it shows whether a program is working, and where to move money and people: a target falling behind, a town whose enrolment jumped, a road paid for and not finished.
- **It shows what to improve.** Compared across towns and years, it shows what worked where, so the next plan builds on what succeeded instead of repeating what did not.

{{figure:shows}}

That holds in every sector: for schools and children, for farms, roads and water, for families in need, and for disaster relief. To see what it takes, and how far the region's numbers are from it, follow one example through: health.

## One example: where should the next health station go?

In health, a planner's question each year is simple: where should the next barangay health station go? To answer it, the data has to show the same three things:

- **What is lacking:** which barangays have no station, and how many people live there.
- **What to adjust:** which stations need help, with no midwife or no medicine.
- **What to improve:** which towns are reaching more mothers and children, so the others can learn from them.

For that, a planner needs a list of every facility, kept current, and a count of the people each one serves, from the same year and on the same map. Here is what exists.

## The newest list is five years old

The newest list of the region's health facilities, town by town, is the Department of Health's National Health Facility Registry as of January 27, 2021. It counts 842 facilities in the region's 100 towns of the time: 543 barangay health stations, 108 rural health units, 29 hospitals, and birthing homes, infirmaries and others.

{{figure:health-list}}

Since then, the map has moved. Maguindanao split into Maguindanao del Norte and del Sur in 2022. Sulu left the region in 2024, and the same year the Special Geographic Area's 8 towns were created from barangays that had belonged to towns of North Cotabato. The 2021 list has no row for any of the 8; in the 2024 census they hold 191,235 people. A list drawn on the old lines has to be redrawn onto the new map, barangay by barangay, before it can be compared with anything newer. Most planners do not have the time, so the numbers are used as they are, and the plan inherits the error.

Health is not alone. Across sectors, the newest figure a planner can find is often years old, or not in public at all.

{{figure:ages}}

The plan's targets are checked against figures from 2024 and 2025; none yet from 2026. Several of the plan's own baselines still include Sulu. And no office keeps a public register of road crashes, drownings and fires: [Incident Watch](https://betterbarmm.com/accidents) has to read them from the news.

Some records do show what current looks like. Every town's income and spending reaches the Bureau of Local Government Finance each year, and the [Local Government](https://lgu.betterbarmm.com) workspace holds them up to 2025. More than 100,000 bid notices are on PhilGEPS since 2019, in the [Procurement](https://procurement.betterbarmm.com) workspace. And the Department of Education's [school-level files](https://www.deped.gov.ph/machine-ready-files/) run to school year 2025–26. What those have in common: one office counts the same thing every period, publishes it, and ties each row to a code, so this year can be compared with last.

## The real problem: numbers years apart

Old figures are one problem. The bigger one is that the figures a plan needs are from different years, and a plan has to put them together.

A plan rarely uses a number on its own. It divides one by another: people per health station, learners per classroom, the poor per barangay. The region's people were counted again in 2024: 4,516,109 of them, in 2,174 barangays. Divide them by the 543 barangay health stations of 2021, and you get about 8,300 people per station.

{{figure:ratio}}

That number describes no real year. Its people are from 2024 and its stations from 2021, and some of those people live in towns the 2021 list does not have. You cannot plan for 2026 with data from 2021. The same holds for every sector at once: a town's schools, health stations, roads, water and disaster plans all need numbers from the same year, because the plan has to weigh them against each other.

{{figure:apart}}

The fix is not one new survey. It is counting the main things on the same cycle, so that each year's plan can draw its figures from the same year, on the same map.

## When the numbers disagree

Even the figures that exist can disagree. For child stunting in 2023, the plan's own tracker, from the health offices' weighing records, reports 21%. The national nutrition survey of 2023, as cited in the plan's midterm update, reports 34.3%. Two official answers for one year, 13 points apart.

{{figure:stunting}}

Neither is wrong: one comes from records health offices keep, the other from a national survey of households. But the plan's target was set on the first, and its latest reading is the second, so the two cannot be compared.

Other sectors have the same problem. Ask how many learners were in school in the Bangsamoro in school year 2023–24, and the official answers differ by nearly 200,000.

{{figure:three-counts}}

Only one of the three comes with the schools it adds up, so only that one can be checked, broken down by town, or compared with next year. A figure in a speech, a press release or a poster can be valuable, and sometimes it is found nowhere else. But it cannot be added up, joined to a town or checked against last year's.

## Gaps found late

When numbers are kept but not published as they come in, problems surface late. Infants fully vaccinated before their first birthday fell from 60.7% in 2021 to 43.8% in 2023, and reached 51.6% in 2024, against a target of 75%. The plan's 2024 report names a cause: national vaccine stock-outs.

{{figure:vaccines}}

A count published as it comes in, town by town, is what lets a gap like that be caught, and adjusted, while there is still time.

Other sectors show the same. The audits of the region's towns are a measure of their records.

{{figure:audits}}

Of the 55 town audits for FY 2020 that this site could read, only 2 got a clean opinion. In 35, the auditors' main findings mention the inventory of the town's own property or supplies: a town that cannot count what it owns cannot plan what it needs. No report on a regional ministry could be read for any year from 2019 to 2024. And in May 2026, the Bangsamoro Parliament's Committee on Finance, Budget and Management reviewed the education budget and found shortfalls no public register had shown before ([Daily Tribune](https://tribune.net.ph/2026/05/28/barmm-budget-review-exposes-gaps-in-bangsamoro-education-sector), [Newsline](https://newsline.ph/audit-exposes-deepening-education-crisis-in-barmm/)).

{{figure:gaps}}

Each of those is a planning question, and every sector has its own version. How many health stations lack a midwife? Which barangays have no evacuation site? Which roads were paid for and not finished? With a register that counts them every year, place by place, the answer is a search away: what is lacking shows before the budget is drawn up, and a shortfall is caught in March, while it can still be adjusted, instead of at a hearing in May.

## Shared, data does more

Data does even more when offices share it. A single family can appear on several government lists, each kept its own way: the health office's, the schools', social welfare's, the planners'. When those lists use the same place codes and the same IDs:

- **A family is counted once,** not four times, and every office's count matches the others'.
- **Each office sees what the others already know.** The health office can see which barangays have the most young children; relief can reach the families social welfare already knows.
- **Help reaches people sooner,** because no one has to start a list from nothing when a need arises.
- **No office pays to collect the same numbers twice.**

{{figure:shared}}

That is what the fourth of the five steps below, joining the data, makes possible.

## Five steps to numbers the region can plan on

Between a figure kept in an office and a figure a planner, a reporter or a resident can use, there are five steps: collect it, publish it, keep it current, join it to other records, and open it for reuse. The region is partway up. Three of the five are in place in part, two have not started, and none is complete.

{{figure:ladder}}

None of the five needs new technology. Each needs a decision, and each one lets the region see more clearly what is lacking, what to adjust and what to improve:

1. **Collect: count the same things every year,** with the same definitions: every health facility and its staff, every school's learners, every project's progress. This is what shows whether a program is on track, in time to adjust it.
2. **Publish: put what is counted where anyone can find it,** in files people can download, not only in Acts, speeches, releases and posters. This is what lets a gap be seen by more than the office that counted it.
3. **Keep it current: update on a schedule, and date every figure,** with the year and places it covers, and whether Sulu is in it. This is what keeps a planner in 2026 from working from a list of 2021.
4. **Join it: use the codes that already exist,** the Philippine Statistics Authority's place codes and IDs like the school ID, so a town's schools, clinics, roads and budget line up, and offices can share one list. This is what turns separate counts into the ratios a plan needs, like people per health station or learners per classroom.
5. **Open it: say how the data may be reused,** under clear terms, so researchers, citizens and other offices can build on it and point to what could be done better.

National agencies have shown how quickly this can move. The Department of Education went from reports to downloadable school files in a single release, and every town's finances already reach the Bureau of Local Government Finance each year. The region can do the same for what only it holds: its ministries' programs and spending, its health stations and their staff, its madaris and learning centres, its scholarships, its projects under construction, and its own audits.

## What BetterBARMM does, and does not do

BetterBARMM gathers what the region's offices and the national government publish, puts it side by side, and links every figure back to its source. It does not make the figures: every number here is the source's own, shown as published, with its date. Where a record is old, missing or in disagreement, we say so, as this explainer does.

The faster the region publishes, the more current this site can be. Every dataset an office opens is one more question a resident, a farmer, a teacher or a planner can answer for themselves, and one more place where the next plan can be built on what is true now rather than on what was true years ago.
